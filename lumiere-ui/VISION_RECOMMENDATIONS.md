# Photo analysis: what this demo can say

The integrated GitHub project is **classical computer vision**. Its Haar cascade finds a face; its `skin_analysis.py` returns the mean HSV brightness, mean red channel and grayscale variance over the detected face rectangle. There is no LLM, CNN, face mesh or trained skin-condition classifier in that project. The crop includes eyes, lips, hair and background as well as skin. Its former 0–10 scores and rule-based product matching had no demonstrated calibration, so Lumière does not use them.

Lumière currently adds one-face checks, blur and exposure hints, a small geometric cheek color ratio and explicit photo-retention choice. These measurements can make a hackathon demo engaging and can support conversations about **visible differences under comparable lighting**. They cannot say why skin is red, painful or flaky; measure skin hydration; diagnose acne or dermatitis; assign a reliable “skin health” score; or prove a product caused improvement. The journal therefore graphs **user-rated** comfort, redness, blemishes and tone and overlays confirmed product and routine changes. A change marker is context, not causal evidence.

Qwen3 8B in this app is **text-only**. It receives the verified measurement summary and self-report, not the uploaded image. Adding an LLM alone would improve wording and follow-up questions, but it would not improve the validity of the image measurements. A vision-language model could describe visible features, but it would still require external validation and careful limits before giving individualized skin-status labels.

For the next version, prioritize:

1. **Better image capture:** consistent distance and diffuse light, no filters, checks for blur, glare, occlusion and multiple faces; show a clear “cannot assess” state. For comparisons, ask for similar lighting and camera conditions.
2. **Anatomically relevant pixels:** use tested face landmarks and skin segmentation so hair, eyes, lips and background do not dominate signals. Measure defined regions and record uncertainty.
3. **Validated labels:** decide one narrow cosmetic question, such as whether a human reviewer can consistently label visible redness in a region. Build a consented, diverse dataset of ordinary phone photos with expert annotations and self-reported symptoms. Evaluate held-out people and devices, false reassurance, skin-tone differences and lighting conditions. Report metrics and uncertainty; do not train to arbitrary scores.
4. **Human boundary:** keep pain, itch, stinging, duration, recent products and red-flag symptoms as conversation questions. Route persistent, severe or worsening symptoms to a qualified professional. Never override this boundary with a photo or product sale.
5. **Product catalogue:** maintain current ingredient lists, label directions, market availability and reviewed suitability rules. Compare budget only with verified local prices. Let users say a product did not suit them, and do not keep suggesting it.

The [OpenCV cascade documentation](https://docs.opencv.org/4.12.0/db/d28/tutorial_cascade_classifier.html) explains the face detector. Published [Diverse Dermatology Images research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35960806/) found meaningful performance gaps on darker skin tones and uncommon conditions, even for trained dermatology models; this underlines why a simple unvalidated photo pipeline should stay descriptive. The [American Academy of Dermatology’s dry-skin guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/dermatologists-tips-relieve-dry-skin) supports simple care guidance and professional review when symptoms persist.
